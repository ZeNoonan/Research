Six chips in a hat: pricing the Rouge et Noir security

Roll a die, hide the result, fill a hat with black and red chips, and auction a security that pays $100 whenever a black chip comes out. This page shows — state by state, fraction by fraction — how the fair value of that security is calculated, why the probabilities trip nearly everyone up, and what happened when 96 professional risk managers played it for fake money.

The game

Setup (done once, in secret). Roll a standard six-sided die; call the result D. Put D black chips into a hat, then add red chips until the hat holds exactly six. Nobody playing knows D — they only know the procedure.

Six rounds. Each round, every team submits a sealed bid for one security: it pays $100 if the next chip drawn from the hat is black, $0 if it is red. The median bid becomes the price: teams that bid above the median buy at the median, teams below it sell at the median, and a team exactly at the median doesn’t trade. Then a chip is drawn, shown to everyone, and set aside — the hat shrinks by one chip each round, so the six draws eventually reveal the entire hat.

Strategy. There is nothing game-theoretic to outguess: your bid can’t improve the price you trade at, only which side of it you end up on. The optimal play is simply to bid the security’s expected value. The whole game is therefore one repeated math problem: given the chips seen so far, what is the probability the next chip is black?

That math problem is where everyone’s intuition snags, because two different kinds of information arrive with every draw. Before tackling the full game, here is a version small enough to hold entirely in your head.

Warm-up: the three-chip version

Same game, shrunk: flip a coin instead of rolling a die. Heads → put 2 black + 1 red chips in the hat; tails → 1 black + 2 red. Three chips, three rounds, and the hidden number of blacks D is 1 or 2 with equal probability. The security still pays $100 on black.

Round 1 — average over what the coin might have done

No chips seen yet, so weight each coin outcome equally:

P(black) = ½ · 13 + ½ · 23 = 12   ⇒  value = $50.00

Round 2 — the part that trips people up

Say the first chip drawn is black . Good news for the security, surely — a black draw hints the hat is the 2-black one? Let’s do it properly. Two things must be combined:

  • Learning (Bayes): a black first draw is twice as likely from the 2-black hat (chance 2/3) as from the 1-black hat (chance 1/3), so the posterior odds tilt to P(D=2) = 2/3, P(D=1) = 1/3.
  • Depletion: that black chip is gone. If D=1 the hat now holds 0 black of 2; if D=2 it holds 1 black of 2.

P(black next) = 13 · 0 + 23 · 12 = 13   ⇒  value = $33.33

The value fell — from $50.00 to $33.33 — after the good-news draw. Depletion beat learning: the coin can only spread the hat between 1 and 2 blacks, so seeing a black tells you little, but removing one from a three-chip hat matters a lot. By symmetry, drawing a red chip first sends the value up to $66.67.

Round 3 — certainty at the edges

  • Seen (2 black): the hat must have been the 2-black one and both blacks are gone — last chip is certainly red. Value $0.00.
  • Seen (2 red): must be the 1-black hat, and its black is still inside — last chip is certainly black. Value $100.00.
  • Seen one of each: both hats explain this equally well (each with probability 1/3 for a specific order), so the posterior stays 50/50 — and the last chip is black exactly when D=2. Value $50.00.

The two forces, named. Every draw in this family of games pushes the value two ways at once:

① Learning — a black draw is evidence the hat started with more blacks (value ↑).
② Depletion — a black draw removes a black from the hat (value ↓).

In the three-chip game depletion wins. In the six-chip game the die ranges over 1–6, so a draw carries much more evidence and learning usually wins — but not always, which is exactly where the full game gets interesting.

Sanity check before any draws: unconditionally, every draw — first, third, or last — has the same 50% chance of being black (58.33% in the six-chip game). Symmetry ("exchangeability") guarantees it; only conditioning on what you’ve seen moves the value.

The recipe for the full game

Back to six chips and a die. Suppose you have watched B black and R red chips come out of the hat. Five steps price the security:

1

List the die rolls still possible. The hat produced B blacks, so D ≥ B (and the die shows at least 1); it produced R reds, so 6 − D ≥ R, i.e. D ≤ 6 − R.

2

How well does each roll explain what you saw? For each candidate D, the probability that the first B+R draws contained exactly B blacks is the hypergeometric count — ways to pick the blacks from D, times ways to pick the reds from 6−D, over ways to pick any B+R chips from 6:

P(seen | D) = C(D, B) · C(6−D, R)C(6, B+R)

C(n, k) is “n choose k”. The order of the observed draws doesn’t matter — every order with the same counts is equally likely, so it cancels.

3

Update the die (Bayes). All six rolls started equally likely, so the posterior probability of each surviving D is just its likelihood from step 2 divided by the sum of the likelihoods of all surviving rolls.

4

Count what’s left in the hat. If the roll was D, the hat still holds D−B blacks out of 6−B−R chips, so P(next black | D) = (D−B) / (6−B−R). This is the depletion step people forget.

5

Average. Value = $100 × ΣD [posterior of D] × [P(next black | D)].

The five classic mistakes (the reasons “the probabilities really trip you up”):

  • Skipping the update: keeping P(black) = 58.33% forever, as if draws taught nothing.
  • Skipping depletion: updating beliefs about D but computing D/6 instead of (D−B)/(6−B−R) — the drawn chips are out of the hat.
  • Using a point estimate: picking the single most likely D (or the observed frequency B/(B+R)) instead of averaging over the whole posterior.
  • Forgetting the die has no zero: D ≥ 1 means the hat is guaranteed at least one black chip — the source of the strangest row in the table below.
  • Gambler’s-fallacy shortcuts: “lots of reds, black is due” is wrong reasoning here — yet in one regime it accidentally points the right way, which makes the wrong habit feel validated.

The hidden pattern in the table

Stare at the triangle and two curiosities emerge — both were deliberate trip-wires in Brown’s design.

1. Once a single black has shown, the answer is Laplace’s rule of succession

For every state with B ≥ 1, the five-step calculation collapses to a formula famous from a different problem entirely:

value = $100 · B + 1B + R + 2

Check it: after 1 black and 2 reds, (1+1)/(3+2) = 2/5 → $40.00. After 3 blacks and 1 red, 4/6 → $66.67. This is Laplace’s rule of succession — the 18th-century formula for “probability the sun rises tomorrow given it rose n days in a row” — which arises whenever you observe successes and failures under a uniform prior. A uniform die over hat compositions plus drawing without replacement gives exactly the same predictive probabilities as the classic coin-with-unknown-bias setup. (The verification script in this folder, rouge_et_noir.py, proves the identity exactly for all fifteen B ≥ 1 states.)

2. The all-red column runs backwards

The exception is the left edge, B = 0, where the die’s missing zero bites:

Early reds are bad news (learning: the hat is probably red-heavy), but the die guarantees at least one black chip is in there somewhere. As reds pile up, that guaranteed black becomes a larger and larger share of a shrinking hat, until after five reds the last chip is black with certainty. The value falls, bottoms at $43.75, then climbs to $100 — so from this column, a red draw raises the value and a black draw (to $33.33, via the rule of succession) lowers it, the mirror image of everywhere else. Brown: “this introduces a complexity into the bidding strategy.” It’s the three-chip game’s lesson resurfacing: when learning has nothing left to teach, depletion runs the price.

What happened at GARP

Brown ran this at the 2013 GARP (Global Association of Risk Professionals) convention in New York: 96 attendees in 16 teams, playing for fake money. The die roll turned out to be D = 1 — one lonely black chip — and the hat delivered Red, Black, Red, Red, Red, Red. So the fair value slid from $58.33 down to $28.57, with one bump to $50 after the black.

Round 1 was a wisdom-of-crowds success: the median bid was almost exactly the correct $58.33. Everything after that went sideways — the mean bid moved in the opposite direction to the fair value in every single round. A red draw lowered the value; bids rose. The black draw raised it; bids fell. By round 6 the mean bid was around $87 for a security worth $28.57 — the widest gap of the game, on the round where the math was nearly at its easiest.

The explanation pops out when you split each round’s bidders by whether they won or lost money the round before:

Winners stayed sane; losers went rogue. Teams that had just won bid close to fair value and adjusted in the right direction. Teams that had just lost look, in Brown’s words, “wildly irrational”: those who lost money buying bid considerably more the next round; the round where sellers lost (round 2, the black draw), losers bid considerably less. That is loss-chasing — doubling down on the trade that just failed, in the superstitious belief that a desired event which didn’t happen is now more likely. It’s doubly irrational here: with everyone free to bid the same number, wins and losses were pure luck, and last round’s luck says nothing about next round’s. And since teams bounced randomly between the winner and loser groups, it can’t be a skill difference — the same people computed calmly after a win and chased after a loss.

“Recall that these are quantitative risk managers, not cowboy traders, playing anonymously for fake money at a conference among friends… Simply by putting people in groups and introducing some competition, we were able to induce behavior more often associated with rogue traders than sober risk professionals. If we can do that, imagine what the markets and real money and frenetic trading rooms can do to impressionable junior traders.” — Aaron Brown

Takeaways — and what this security rhymes with

  • Losses switch off arithmetic. The skill was in the room all six rounds — the median team priced round 1 perfectly. What changed after losses wasn’t ability but the willingness to use it. Process discipline has to be designed for the moments after a loss, because that’s precisely when people abandon it.
  • P&L is not information. Teams reacted to a factor (last round’s luck) that was doubly irrelevant — and reacted in the direction of chasing it. Judge decisions by the quality of the pricing, never by the outcome of the draw.
  • Crowds are wise until they start keeping score. One round of wins and losses was enough to turn independent estimates into correlated emotion.
  • Complexity wasn’t the trigger. The round-6 math is nearly the easiest in the game and produced the worst pricing. If six chips in a hat can do this, real books with real money need structural guardrails, not smarter people.

Things it should remind you of

  • A binary option / prediction-market contract. Pays a fixed $100 or nothing on an event — exactly a digital option or a Polymarket/Kalshi/Iowa-Electronic-Markets contract; the fair bid is the event probability × $100, marked to market as information arrives.
  • Laplace’s rule of succession & Pólya’s urn. As shown above, the whole B ≥ 1 table is (B+1)/(B+R+2) — Bayesian updating with a uniform prior in its oldest costume.
  • Card counting. Value driven by the depleting composition of a finite shoe — except here the deck’s composition is itself unknown, so you run blackjack’s running count and a Bayesian estimate of the shoe at the same time.
  • Pull-to-par. The all-red column is a distressed bond limping toward maturity: each period of bad news survived leaves the guaranteed payoff a bigger share of a shorter future.
  • Experimental asset-market bubbles. A room full of subjects bidding a known, declining fundamental far above value is the classic Smith–Suchanek–Williams bubble result, reproduced here in six rounds flat.
  • Rogue traders. Loss-chasing — size up after losing to get back to even — is the signature of Leeson and Kerviel, and of the break-even effect Kahneman and Tversky documented: people who are down accept gambles they would never otherwise touch.
