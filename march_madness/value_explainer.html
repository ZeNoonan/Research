Part 1The game you’re actually playing

A bracket pool pays the top of the standings, not the middle. Picking every favorite maximises your expected score — and lands you 60th–80th percentile: good, never first. To finish first out of 100 you need your score to land far above the pool average, and that means you need variance — deliberate, cheap variance.

The two curves below are the model’s estimate of where your score lands relative to the pool average, for the sheet’s default all-chalk bracket and for the same bracket with Michigan State picked to win it all. The MSU bracket’s centre is lower (it costs expected points) but its tails are fatter — and pools are won in the right tail.

Part 2The crowd’s map — probability vs pick frequency

Value exists because the crowd’s picks don’t match the probabilities. In 2019 the crowd was drunk on Zion-era Duke: 42.4% of pools picked Duke as champion against a model probability of 18.4% — backed at 2.3× its probability. That over-backing had to come from somewhere: Virginia (picked at 0.4× its probability), Gonzaga (0.5×) and Michigan State (0.6×) were all left under-owned. North Carolina shows the opposite: a brand the crowd over-backed — no value there.

Part 3Slots, not teams

The spreadsheet doesn’t evaluate “Michigan State” — it evaluates 63 independent decisions called slots: each round-cell of your bracket. In the round of 64 a slot has 2 candidate teams; each later round doubles the candidates until the champion slot has all 64. For every slot, the baseline is the favorite — the most likely winner, which for MSU’s first three slots is MSU itself, and from the Elite 8 onward is Duke.

Picking MSU in a slot where MSU is the favorite costs nothing — the Value flag is automatic (the sheet shows ratio 0). The real question starts at the Elite 8: MSU’s probability of reaching the Final Four is 0.22 against Duke’s 0.53, and the slot is worth 8 points. Picking MSU there gives up 8 × (0.22 − 0.53) = -2.48 expected points. What do you get back?

Part 4The variance-difference formula, term by term

What you get back is variance against the field. The score that decides the pool isn’t yours — it’s yours minus everyone else’s average. Whenever you and the crowd pick the same team, that game cancels out of the comparison; whenever you differ, the game swings the gap both ways. The spreadsheet’s AL:AQ columns compute how much the spread of that gap grows when you pick team i instead of the favorite:

ΔVar = pts² · [ (p−p₀)(1+2S) + (p₀²−p²) + 2(p₀f₀−pf) ]
-0.574you win this slot less often, so it wobbles your total less — a variance loss
+0.233the squared-probability correction from Bernoulli variance p(1−p)
+0.709the crowd term: the favorite moves with the field (f₀ 0.73); your pick moves against it (f 0.15)
= +0.368per point² — times 8² = +23.5 for the Elite-8 slot

The one-sentence intuition: when 73% of the pool rides Duke and Duke loses, all those brackets sink together — and your MSU bracket rockets past the whole group at once. Variance against the field is cheap precisely where the crowd is most concentrated.

Where the formula comes from (derivation sketch)

Give each slot 1 point. Your slot score is a Bernoulli Xi with P(Xi=1) = pi; the field’s average score on the slot is A = Σj fjXj with E[A] = S. Working out Var(Xi − A) — noting the Xj of one slot are mutually exclusive, and Cov(Xi, A) = pifi − piS — gives the paper’s expression pi(2S+1−pi−2fi) − S² + S. Subtract the same expression for the favorite (p₀, f₀) and the −S²+S parts cancel, leaving exactly the three terms above. Points multiply variance by pts².

Part 5Accumulate, divide, test: the ratio rule

A champion pick implies winning six slots, so the sheet accumulates both sides across rounds: cumulative expected points given up and cumulative variance gained, then divides them. The rule (“lower limit of 1 on the ratio” in the paper): a pick earns the Value flag when it’s the favorite, or when variance gained ≥ points given up — with the sheet’s sign convention (given-up points are stored negative), a ratio ≤ −1.

Part 6The whole field on one map

Run the same two cumulative numbers for all 64 teams in the champion column and the definition becomes a picture: a team is a champion-column Value pick when it sits above the gain = give-up line. Virginia is the standout — a co-favorite by probability, abandoned by the crowd, so it buys 51 variance for 0.4 points. North Carolina and Houston sit low: the crowd already over-backs them, so deviating to them buys little spread or even reduces it.

Value ≠ longshot. The eight champion Value picks of 2019 were Duke, Virginia, Gonzaga, Kentucky, Michigan St, Michigan, Texas Tech and Virginia Tech — favorites and second favorites the crowd under-owned, not 12 seeds. Longshots fail the test from both sides: tiny p buys almost no variance, and still costs the full EV gap. The 2019 title game was Virginia – Texas Tech, with MSU in the Final Four.

Part 7Does the trade actually pay?

Honesty first: under the model’s own assumptions, in a 100-entry pool, the default Duke bracket still edges the MSU bracket (2.28× vs 2.01× a random entry) — 7½ points of expected value is a lot to give back. But variance scales with pool size, because the winning score climbs as the field grows. Two things follow. First, the near-free value pick — swapping the champion cell to Virginia costs 0.41 points for +51 variance — beats chalk at every pool size. Second, the expensive MSU version overtakes chalk once the pool passes roughly 1,102 entries.

And remember the Read Me’s warning: these curves assume the probability and frequency tables are exactly right. The entire reason Value picks exist is that you believe the crowd’s frequencies are wrong in knowable ways — so treat the estimate as a compass, not a payout table. The paper’s strongest empirical claim stands separately: all 32 tournament champions to that point had been Value picks.

Part 8What to remember

  • Pools pay position, not points. Expected value gets you to the 70th percentile; variance against the field is what gets you to 1st.
  • The favorite is always a Value pick (ratio 0). Value is about which deviations from chalk are efficiently priced.
  • The crowd term drives everything. ΔVar is dominated by 2(p₀f₀ − pf): deviation is cheapest exactly where the field is most herded (Duke 2019 at 42% champion ownership).
  • Second favorites, not longshots. A value pick needs real win probability and low crowd ownership. UNC (brand-inflated) failed; Virginia (brand-deflated co-favorite) was the trade of the year.
  • Pool size sets the variance budget. Small pool → shade toward chalk; big pool → every efficient variance trade you can find. The MSU-style deep run only pays past ~1k entries; the Virginia-style free swap pays everywhere.
  • Caveats: the machinery is Gaussian and treats slots as independent; in “must-win” situations (huge pools, top-heavy scoring) the metric shades toward p/f; and none of it accounts for rivals using the same sheet. Also: this page uses the fixed formulas — as published, the reference bug (S from 38 rows down) and the reversed ratio test meant the Value column flagged almost none of this.
