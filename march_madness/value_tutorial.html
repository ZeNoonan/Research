The idea in one paragraph

A bracket pool pays the winner, not the average scorer — so the right question is never “which picks score the most points on average?” but “which picks give me the best chance of finishing first?”. Those are different questions, and variance is the difference between them. The Value factor awards a star to a pick that increases the variance of your score against the pool by at least as much as it costs in expected points — variance bought at a fair price or better. The slot favorite gets the star automatically (it costs nothing to pick). It is the bracket version of the value factor in Fama–French investing: don’t pay for what’s popular, buy what’s cheap per unit of what you actually need — and what you need in a pool is variance.

Why variance wins pools

Suppose you copy the crowd’s favorite in every slot. Your score then moves almost in lock-step with the pool average: when your teams win, everyone’s teams win. You’ll finish mid-pack nearly every year — a high expected score, but almost no chance of being first in a pool of 100, because dozens of near-identical brackets sit ahead of you on any tiebreak of luck.

To finish first you must be different from the field where being different is cheap, so that some plausible run of results catapults you past the crowd. Every deviation from the favorites costs expected points — the favorite is, by definition, the most likely winner — but the right deviations buy a lot of spread between you and the field for very little expected cost. The Value factor is exactly that price check, run slot by slot: how many points of expected score am I paying, and how much score-variance against the pool am I getting?

The same model uses this machinery a second time: the main page’s pool win-probability block treats your score minus the field’s as a Gaussian and integrates it against the best of the other entries. More variance widens that Gaussian — and when you need to beat 99 other brackets, the right tail is where wins come from.

The pieces the formula is built from

The tournament is scored in six rounds with points 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21 per correct pick (editable on the main page; fixed here for clarity). For round k, the 64 teams split into groups of 2k+1: pairs for the Round of 64, fours for the Round of 32, eights for the Sweet 16 … all 64 for the Champion slot. Exactly one team from each group wins that slot and scores its backers the round’s points. Everything below is computed per slot, always relative to that group of teams.

p — win probability

The chance your pick wins this slot (reaches the round and wins it). Workbook tab “Brackets - prob”.

f — pick frequency

The share of pool entries that picked this team for this slot (ESPN “Who Picked Whom” data). Tab “Brackets - freq”.

p₀, f₀ — the favorite

p₀ is the highest win probability in the group; f₀ is that team’s pick frequency (averaged if several teams tie for the top probability).

S = Σ p₁f₁ — the field’s slot rate

Sum of p·f over the whole group: the probability that a randomly drawn pool entry scores this slot. Column B of the “Slot calculations” tab — the cell the fill-down bug mis-references.

The opponent model throughout: a random pool entry picks team j in this slot with probability fj. That single random opponent stands in for the whole field, slot by slot.

Step 1Your score swing against a random opponent

Fix one slot and compare yourself with one random opponent. Let Xj be 1 if team j wins the slot (0 otherwise). You picked team i; the opponent picked team J, drawn with the pick frequencies. Your swing on this slot is the score difference

Y = Xi − XJ ∈ {−1, 0, +1} (in units of the round’s points)

Y can only be +1 (your team won, theirs didn’t), −1 (theirs won, yours didn’t) or 0 (same pick, or both wrong). Its moments come straight from enumerating who-wins × who-they-picked:

E[Y] = p − S P(Y = +1) = p·(1 − f) you win, opponent is on someone else P(Y = −1) = S − p·f a team the opponent backs wins, and it isn’t yours E[Y²] = P(+1) + P(−1) = p − 2pf + S Var(Y) = E[Y²] − E[Y]² = p − 2pf + S − (p − S)² = p(1 − p − 2f + 2S) + S − S²

That last line is exactly the workbook’s “Variance” columns (AF:AK of the Slot calculations tab), one cell per team per round — and the quantity the whole Value factor is built on. Note what pushes it up: a high win probability p paired with a low pick frequency f (the −2pf term). Being right where the crowd isn’t is what creates spread.

Step 2What a non-favorite pick costs: ΔEV

The baseline pick for any slot is the favorite (probability p₀). Swapping it for team i changes your expected slot score by

ΔEV = pts · (p − p₀) ≤ 0 for every non-favorite

The opponent’s side (the −S in E[Y]) is the same whichever team you pick, so it cancels: the expected cost of a deviation is just the probability gap times the round’s points. This is the workbook’s “Expected points difference” (columns T:Y), and the sheet stores it as this negative number — remember that sign, it decides the direction of the final inequality.

Step 3What the pick buys: ΔVar

Now do the same swap for the variance. Both picks face the same slot, so S is the same in both expressions and most terms cancel:

ΔVar/pts² = Var(pick i) − Var(pick favorite) = [p − p² − 2pf + 2pS] − [p₀ − p₀² − 2p₀f₀ + 2p₀S] (S − S² cancels) = (p − p₀) − (p² − p₀²) − 2(pf − p₀f₀) + 2S(p − p₀) = (p − p₀)(1 + 2S) − p² + p₀² − 2(pf − p₀f₀) ΔVar = pts² × that bracket (variance scales with the square of the stakes)

This is the paper’s formula and the workbook’s “Variance difference” columns (AL:AQ). Read the three parts: the (p − p₀)(1 + 2S) term is the raw probability gap amplified by how much the field already scores in this slot; −p² + p₀² trims each side’s self-variance; and −2(pf − p₀f₀) is the crowding term — the favorite is usually heavily picked (p₀f₀ large), so stepping away from it adds variance, while picking an over-backed dark horse subtracts it.

Where the bug lives: S enters this formula once, through the cross-term — and it must be this slot’s S, the value already sitting in column B of the same row. The published workbook reads it 38 rows down (AL3 says B41), so from row 29 onward the reference walks off the data and Excel silently uses S = 0. The explorer below can replay that.

Step 4Accumulate the rounds, then take the ratio

Picking a team to win round k implies picking it in every earlier round too, so the workbook sums the round-by-round differences down the team’s path — cumulative ΔEV (columns Z:AE) and cumulative ΔVar (AR:AW) — and prices one against the other:

ratio = cum ΔVar / cum ΔEV (columns AX:BC) Value ★ ⇔ favorite (cum ΔEV = 0, ratio defined as 0) or ratio ≤ −1

Why ≤ −1? The rule is “variance gained ≥ expected points lost”, i.e. cum ΔVar ≥ −cum ΔEV. Divide both sides by cum ΔEV — a negative number for any non-favorite — and the inequality flips: cum ΔVar / cum ΔEV ≤ −1. One point of expected score must buy at least one point² of variance; the threshold is an exchange rate, set to 1 on this points scale.

The published workbook tests ratio > 1 instead (bug 2). With a negative denominator, ratio > 1 means the variance change is more negative than the EV change — the flag fired for picks that lose variance faster than they lose expected points, the exact opposite of the paper’s stated rule.

Fine print — the details that keep the calculation honest

  • The favorite can change round to round. Texas Tech is its own group’s favorite in the first two rounds, then compares against Michigan (Sweet 16), Gonzaga (Elite 8) and Duke (Final 4 and Champion). The cumulative sums always price your path against each round’s favorite.
  • Ties for favorite: if several teams share the top probability, f₀ is the average of their pick frequencies (the workbook’s AVERAGEIF), and each of them counts as a favorite (ΔEV = 0, automatic star).
  • The opponent model is an approximation. One random entry drawn from the pick frequencies, slots treated as independent when variances are summed across rounds. The workbook makes the same Gaussian-style approximations in its pool win-probability block; they are excellent for pricing picks, not exact bracket combinatorics.
  • Units and the exchange rate. ΔEV is in points, ΔVar in points², so the ratio is in points — and the −1 threshold is a modeling choice tied to this points scale (2, 3, 5, 8,  13, 21). Rescale the round points and picks move across the boundary; that’s a feature of the workbook, not a bug.
  • ΔVar can be negative. An over-picked underdog separates you from nobody: you pay expected points and lose variance. The frontier chart’s bottom-left shows them.
  • Why favorites star automatically: the factor asks “is this deviation worth it?”; not deviating is always worth it, so the favorite’s ratio is defined as 0 and flagged. Per the paper, every champion from 1985–2018 was a Value pick; with the fixes, the 2019 champion column stars Duke, Virginia Tech, Michigan State, Gonzaga, Texas Tech, Michigan, Virginia and Kentucky — including the actual champion, Virginia.
  • Data quirk: the workbook lists “Mississippi State” twice; the South 8-seed should be Mississippi (Ole Miss). Kept as-is here and on the main page, flagged with *.
