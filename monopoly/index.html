The Papers, and What We Did With Them

Wilmott · Jan & Mar 2003

Monopoly 101 appeared in two parts in Wilmott, the quant-finance magazine. Aaron Brown — then a Wall Street risk manager, later head of financial market research at AQR — used Monopoly as a teaching weapon. His claim: students shrug at the Gordon growth model and the CAPM because the assumptions are visibly false, so show them the same machinery winning a board game and they finally understand what simple models are for.

The papers survive as scans in which every displayed equation is unreadable. That made this project a small act of archaeology, in three moves:

  • Recreate. Rebuild the full apparatus from the surviving prose and tables: the Bank-flow parameter Φ, rent rolls, the wealth differential equation, the property-pricing formula, and the 120-state Markov chain behind the board frequencies.
  • Validate. Recompute all 70 quantities the two papers publish. 68 match outright; the remaining two are documented discrepancies we can explain — including one genuine correction to the paper’s headline number (see the ledger).
  • Extend. An independent full-rules game simulator cross-checks the theory empirically, plays color groups against each other head-to-head, and computes the exact house-by-house investment returns the paper’s linearized model averages away.

Everything regenerates from python3 analysis/run_all.py in the repository; nothing on this page requires trusting us or the papers.

Φ — The Cash Engine

Part I → II

Monopoly has a central bank. Money enters the table when you pass Go, draw a lucky card, or collect a matured building loan; it leaves through taxes, fines and repair assessments. Rent — the thing players obsess over — moves money between players and changes the total not one dollar.

Brown compresses all Bank flows into one number, Φ: the expected net payment from the Bank to the table per roll of the dice. On a naive board where every square is equally likely, the arithmetic fits on a napkin: the average roll advances 7 of 40 squares, so Go pays 200·7/40 = $35.00; the two tax squares drain $6.88; the card decks (32 cards netting +$485) chip in $2.27. Net: $30.40 per roll.

One parameter, five regimes

Expected net Bank payment to the table, $ per dice roll.

“Paper convention” credits Go money for dice movement only — Brown’s own $30.40 uses a 7/40 dice term and nothing else, and $23.62 continues that convention. Cards like Advance to Go and Take a Ride on the Reading also carry the token past Go, and that’s worth $4.40 per roll: full accounting gives $27.89, which our simulator confirms empirically ($27.92 over one million dice rolls). The correction changes no conclusions — but if you use the valuation formula, use the corrected Φ.

Two things about Φ decide the character of every Monopoly game:

  • Jail is monetary policy. With 9.39% of all time spent in jail, Go collections sag and the exact Φ falls well below the napkin figure. Late in the game, staying in jail is a rational yield play — you keep collecting rent while paying none.
  • Buildings shrink the money supply. Two repair cards charge per house and hotel: each house on the board costs the table $0.29 per roll, each hotel $0.96 (we reproduce both to the cent). A fully built two-player endgame runs at Φ ≈ $13 — half the opening subsidy, right when rents are deadliest.
?Simple example — why your family games never ended

The popular house rules — $300 or $400 for landing on Go, taxes and fines paid into a Free Parking jackpot — look like harmless fun. Run them through the same napkin: Φ jumps from $30 to roughly $69 per roll. The Bank is now shoveling money at the table more than twice as fast, every player’s survival cushion more than doubles, and bankruptcy becomes nearly impossible. The game doesn’t end because you changed the money supply until it couldn’t. Brown’s dry verdict: it “lengthens the game considerably and eliminates any advantage to intelligent play.”

Rent Rolls & the Safe Zone

Part I

Your rent roll ℜ is the rent you expect to collect each time an opponent rolls. Bookkeeping over one round of an n-player game gives the identity the whole theory stands on:

net gain per round  =  n·(ℜyou − ℜaverage) + Φ

You only bleed if your rent roll is more than Φ/n below the table average — with four players, $7.60 per roll. Inside that band, the Bank’s drip feed covers your losses forever. Brown calls it the safe zone, and it explains Monopoly’s two personalities:

  • Undeveloped board: nobody can die. All 28 properties held singly produce $547 of rent — an average rent roll of $3.42. Even owning nothing leaves you just $3.42 below average: comfortably inside the $7.60 zone. A propertyless player literally cannot go bankrupt, so undeveloped deeds are — strictly — worthless.
  • Hotels change the physics. Fully developed, the board rents $22,790 — average rent roll $142. The safe zone is now a rounding error, and every roll is live ammunition.

Who leaves the safe zone? Nobody trades, everybody buys

Distribution of (your rent roll − table average) when 28 properties land on random owners and every completed group is built to hotels. 150,000 Monte Carlo allocations.

Mean zero, SD ≈ $28, violently right-skewed (skew +1.9): most players sit slightly below average while a lucky one holds a monster. About a third of players end outside the safe zone (Brown: “about 40%”), and in half of all games at least one player does. Hence the theorem every good player knows in their bones: some players must trade to survive, so everyone must trade to win. Our simulator agrees from the other side: with trading banned, only 60 of 200 full games produced a bankruptcy in 6,000 rolls.

?Simple example — the allowance game

Four kids pass money around a table; a parent tops up whoever rolls the dice by about $30 a round, split $7.60 each. As long as the kids’ side-payments to each other are all within $7.60 a round of each other, everyone’s pile grows forever — the parent outruns the squabbling. That’s undeveloped Monopoly. Now let one kid triple the toll on her squares: the parent’s top-up is suddenly beside the point. That’s hotels.

The Lurking Exponential

Part I

Now allow development. Houses convert wealth into rent-generating capacity: invest a dollar in the right color group and your rent roll rises by βi per roll, forever. Write that into the bookkeeping identity and wealth obeys a linear differential equation whose solutions all share one shape:

W(t)  =  C·eλt + (linear terms),    λ = (n−1)βyou + βopponents

There is no middle. If your constant C is positive, wealth compounds to infinity. If it is negative, the exponential drags you through zero and off the board. Total table wealth grows only linearly (the Bank drips nΦ per round) — the exponential is a fight over shares. Brown, riffing on Julius Caesar: “There is an exponential tide in the affairs of men, which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune; omitted, all the polynomial voyage of their life is bound in shallows and in miseries.”

Three fates, one differential equation

Wealth paths from the Part I dynamics (time in rounds; development reinvested continuously).

Solving the two-player system exactly (our reconstruction — the scan ate Brown’s equations) gives a clean survival criterion: you outlive your opponent precisely when

ℜyou + βyouβyou + βopp·Φ  >  ℜopp + βoppβyou + βopp·Φ

Read it aloud: current rent, plus your share of every dollar the Bank will ever print — shares split in proportion to development power, and summing to exactly one. In 400 randomized trials this predicts the ODE’s winner 100% of the time. Brown’s published version swaps in a single market-wide interest rate (for two players, the geometric mean √(β₁β₂) — we verified that characterization too); that makes it a price rather than a duel-specific score, at the cost of being an approximation (93% agreement). The paper never flags the distinction; consider it flagged.

?Simple example — spotting the tide while it’s slow

“Exponential” does not mean fast. It means the growth rate is proportional to the level. If something went from $1 billion to $2 billion last month and it’s exponential, it exceeds the wealth of the world within a year — so it almost certainly isn’t. But something that went from $1 to $2 might be, and if it keeps doubling for two years that’s a $30 million opportunity only you have noticed. Good Monopoly players ignore this turn’s cash and watch the exponentially slow variables — rent capacity, monopoly formation — that decide the end. So does good finance.

The Pricing Formula

Part I → II

Since the survival measure decides everyone’s fate, it is the natural yardstick of value. Divide by the game’s interest rate β (a dollar of houses returns β per roll to the average player, so β is the opportunity cost of money) and you have the paper’s central result — the price of any Monopoly asset:

Vi  =  1β ( ℜi  +  βi·Φ(n−1)βi + β )

Two familiar faces are hiding in it. Like the Gordon model, it capitalizes an income stream with a growth adjustment under assumptions everyone knows are false. Like the CAPM, it splits value into parts with different portfolio behavior: rent rolls ℜi add up across your deeds, but options βi don’t — you develop your best group first, so only your biggest option really counts. Railroads and utilities can’t be developed at all (βi = 0): they are pure bonds, worth exactly ℜi/β. A hotel-saturated monopoly has spent its option and becomes a bond too.

Price the whole board yourself

Brown’s formula, live. Drag the game’s interest rate through a whole game’s arc — under 1% in the opening, 4–6% by the endgame — and watch value rotate between growth and income.

Undeveloped groups, Brown’s fitted βi, rent rolls from the exact board frequencies. At opening rates the four railroads are the most valuable asset in the game — casual players undervalue them precisely because they can’t imagine a hotel on a train. As money tightens, the option term deflates and big-rent groups take over. Cheap money inflates growth assets first; Monopoly proves it with dice.

?Simple example — the Oriental Avenue auction

First roll of the game: a player lands on Oriental Avenue ($100 list price) and declines it. Auction! Casual players have no idea what to bid — this is exactly the question the formula exists to answer. As a lone deed, Oriental collects $6 on 2.13% of opponent rolls: ℜ ≈ $0.13 a roll — capitalized at opening rates, worth maybe $8. But whoever assembles all three light blues owns a development machine the formula prices near $510. Oriental’s value isn’t its rent; it’s a one-third share of an option — plus veto power over anyone else’s. That is why serious games auction hot and why “I never pay over list” loses to arithmetic.

The Board Is Not Uniform

Part II

Part II replaces the equal-frequency napkin with the real thing: a Markov chain over 120 states — 40 squares × (fresh roll / one double / two doubles), with the never-landed-on “Go To Jail” square recycled to count jail turns, tournament jail rules, and both card decks folded into the transition matrix. Its stationary distribution is the frequency strip in the hero above. What it says:

  • Jail is the center of the Monopoly universe — 9.39% of all time. Three separate mechanisms pump players into it, and optimal late-game play keeps them there.
  • The orange corridor is downstream of everything. Six, eight and nine squares off jail sit St. James, Tennessee and New York — 8.31% of all opponent rolls land on the group, the best in the game.
  • Chance squares barely exist (0.82–1.04%): ten of sixteen cards whisk the token elsewhere, so the squares themselves almost never end a roll.
  • Illinois Avenue (3.00%) is the most-landed street — its own Chance card teleports players there.

Getting this table right is unforgiving — tournament jail states, the doubles ladder, “nearest railroad” cards that pay double, a card that can pass Go twice in one roll. Our chain matches all forty of Brown’s published frequencies to within 0.005 percentage points, and a from-scratch simulator playing the actual rules agrees with the chain to Monte Carlo noise. When three independent constructions of the same object agree to four decimals, the archaeology is over: this is the table.

The frequencies also expose the famous fine print: the four-railroad rent roll of $23.04 per opponent roll decomposes into $21.24 of ordinary landings plus $1.79 from the two “advance to the nearest railroad, pay double” Chance cards — a rent stream most owners never notice they’re collecting.

Growth Stocks & Value Stocks

Part II + Extension

Feed the formula the exact frequencies and Brown’s fitted βi (estimated by iterated endgame simulation) and the board resolves into an asset menu any investor would recognize. We reproduce every number in the paper’s valuation table — one forensic detail: the printed “1.6% / 6%” interest rates are really 1.6123% / 6.031%, which the railroad rows pin down exactly, being pure annuities.

  • Light blue = small-cap growth. βi 7.65%: converts a tiny bankroll into rent faster than anything nearby, but its hotels top out at $1,700 of rent — no staying power.
  • Green = deep value. The worst project on the board (fitted βi 0.77%) but the biggest fully-built annuity ($1,604 at endgame rates). You fund it only when money is cheap and games are long.
  • Orange = quality growth. The best βi on the board (11.89%) and a top-three annuity, sitting on the most-visited corridor. The formula derives what every Monopoly guide asserts.
  • Railroads = the misunderstood bond. Early game, $1,429 — the single most valuable holding. Endgame, $382. Same asset, different interest rate.

The Third House, or the Lie of Linearity

Extension

Part I treats development as a smooth dial: βi dollars of rent roll per dollar invested, at any scale. Real Monopoly climbs a staircase — and the staircase has a famous step. Here is the exact return of each development stage (one house on every street of the group), computed with the true landing frequencies:

Return per dollar, stage by stage

Increase in rent roll (¢ per opponent roll) per dollar spent, adding one house per street. Stage 5 is the hotel. Hover a cell for the numbers.

Every group’s rent schedule kinks violently at the third house — orange stage 3 pays roughly ten times the return of its own hotel stage. Part I’s constant βi is the average of this hump; optimal play rides the hump’s peak: build to three houses everywhere before taking anything to five. It also explains the expert’s dirtiest trick — buying houses you don’t need to exhaust the Bank’s 32-house stock and freeze opponents at stage two, one step short of the payoff.

?Simple example — New York Avenue, house by house

New York Avenue rents $16 bare, $80 with one house, $220 with two, $600 with three, $800 with four, $1,000 with a hotel. Each house costs the same $100 — but house #3 alone adds $380 of rent while house #5 adds $200. Same price, double the product. When returns are that lumpy, an “average return” is a fiction: the whole game is about landing on the right step of the staircase before your money runs out.

The Validation Ledger

Every published number, retried

Seventy quantities appear in the two papers — from the $30.40 napkin Φ to all forty entries of the valuation table. Each one was recomputed from scratch; the full ledger is below. The honest findings:

  • Φ = $23.62 is under-credited. Card-driven Go collections are worth $4.40/roll; the full figure is $27.89 (simulator-confirmed). Replicating the paper’s own convention we get $23.49 — thirteen cents shy, the paper’s unpublished “minor adjustments.”
  • “$144” average developed rent roll should be $142.44 (= 22,790/160). A slip of the pen.
  • The survival criterion’s market rate is an approximation — exact form uses the opponent’s β; Brown’s geometric-mean version picks the same winner 93% of the time. Fine as a price, not an identity.
  • Everything else — frequencies, repair coefficients, rent rolls, valuations, betas, the geometric-mean characterization — reproduces exactly, most of it to the last printed digit.

Lessons Beyond the Board

Synthesis

Brown’s deeper point was never about Monopoly. It’s that 500 million people have absorbed real financial instincts from a box with dice — and that a page of honest algebra can turn those instincts into principles:

1Exponential beats polynomial

Any positive exponential eventually overtakes any power law. The decisive forces are the exponentially slow ones nobody is watching yet — watch doubling, not size.

2There is no safe zone

“Income exceeds expenses” is a linear idea in an exponential world. Every dollar you earn is a cost someone cleverer is engineering away. You survive by riding a curve, not by standing still.

3Value = annuity + option

Cash now, plus the right to invest later. Annuities add across a portfolio; options don’t — only your best one matters. Most mispricing is ignoring one of the two terms.

4Money supply prices everything

Φ is monetary policy. Loose money → low rates → growth assets inflate and nobody fails. Tight money → annuities rule and the weak die. The board teaches QE with a top hat.

5Models are for rationality

The formula’s assumptions are false and Brown says so cheerfully. Its gift is rational, consistent answers where intuition has nothing — which is exactly what the Gordon model and CAPM are for.

6Trade or die

A third of players draw a losing allocation; they must trade to survive, so everyone must trade to win. Markets exist because the alternative is a game nobody can finish.
