⚾ Little League WAR Explorer

An interactive way to understand the WAR / WAA spreadsheet

This rebuilds the WAR (Wins Above Replacement) spreadsheet so you can poke at every number on your phone and watch the answer change. Blue boxes are inputs you control; gray boxes calculate themselves — exactly like the original workbook.

There is no true "replacement player" in Little League. With mandatory-play rules and good athletes who never sign up, the major-league baseline doesn't translate. So we measure against average (Wins Above Average) or against a hypothetical team that wins ~30% of games, and we borrow the parameters from MLB.

This won't be comparable to big-league WAR. A regular MLB player gets ~10× the plate appearances, so their WAR is ~10× bigger. Look at WAR per game or per PA instead. And no stat ports cleanly — hitting .300 in Little League ≠ ready to hit .300 for the Dodgers.

It's fuzzy. Even over a full MLB career, Baseball Reference and FanGraphs disagree. With one Little League season and the issues above, reasonable people will get very different numbers.

The point is to learn the math — not to rate kids who should be playing for fun. Change a weight, watch a kid's WAR move, and ask why.

Stats you type in (straight from GameChanger)

These describe what a kid did at bat or on the mound.

PAPlate Appearance

One turn at bat. Every time the kid steps up to face the pitcher, that's one PA — whether it ends in a hit, a walk, or an out. It's the basic "how many chances did they get" count.

1BSingle

A hit where the batter reaches first base safely. The most common kind of hit.

2BDouble

A hit good enough to reach second base in one go. Better than a single.

3BTriple

A hit good enough to reach third base. Rare and exciting — usually needs real speed.

HRHome Run

The best outcome: the batter rounds all the bases and scores, normally by hitting the ball out of the park. An automatic run.

BB+HBPWalk + Hit By Pitch

Two ways to reach first base without hitting the ball. A walk (BB, "base on balls") is when the pitcher throws four bad pitches, so the batter gets first base free. Hit by pitch (HBP) is exactly that — the pitch hits the batter, who also gets first base. We lump them together.

SBStolen Base

A runner already on base sprints to the next base on their own — no hit needed — and makes it safely. Risky but valuable.

CSCaught Stealing

The runner tried to steal a base but got tagged out. A failed steal — it costs the team, so the model gives it a negative value.

OutOut

A turn at bat that didn't reach base (struck out, caught, thrown out, etc.). The site works this out for you: PA minus all the times they reached base.

IPInnings Pitched

How many innings the kid spent pitching (throwing to the other team's batters). Only matters for pitchers; leave it 0 for everyone else.

RRuns Allowed

While this kid was pitching, how many runs the other team scored. Fewer is better — it's the main measure of how well someone pitched.

Positions (where they stand on the field)

Some spots are much harder than others, so the model hands out a bonus or penalty for each — see "Position runs" below.

PPitcher

Throws the ball to the batter to start every play. The most important defensive job.

CCatcher

Crouches behind home plate and catches every pitch the batter doesn't hit. Physically brutal and skill-heavy — gets the biggest position bonus.

1B / 2B / 3BFirst / Second / Third Base

The three "corners and middle" infield spots, named after the base they guard. First base is the easiest (small bonus or penalty); second and third are tougher.

SSShortstop

Fields the area between second and third base. Lots of action and long throws — one of the hardest spots, so it earns a healthy bonus.

LF / CF / RFLeft / Center / Right Field

The three outfielders, standing far back to chase long hits. Center field covers the most ground (a bonus); the corners are easier (a small penalty).

DHDesignated Hitter

A player who only bats and never fields. Because they skip the hard part, the model subtracts the most runs from them.

The model's terms (what the math builds)

These are the outputs and the ideas behind them — the stuff the gray boxes calculate.

WARWins Above Replacement

The headline number. Roughly: how many extra wins did this kid give the team compared with a freely-available, below-average fill-in player. Bigger = more valuable. A 0.0 means "about as good as a spare-part player."

WAAWins Above Average

The same idea, but measured against an average player instead of a replacement-level one. A tougher bar, so the numbers come out lower.

RARRuns Above Replacement

WAR's twin, but counted in runs instead of wins. We total up every run the kid created at bat and saved while pitching (beyond a replacement player), then convert runs → wins to get WAR.

RPWRuns Per Win

The exchange rate between runs and wins — about 17 runs ≈ 1 win here. Little League is high-scoring, so it takes a lot of runs to "buy" one win. Dividing RAR by RPW gives WAR.

Linear weightsRun values

A simple points system: each good thing (single, walk, home run…) is worth a set number of runs, and each out costs runs. Add them all up and you've valued a hitter in runs.

BatRunsBatting Runs

The total run value of everything the kid did at the plate, using those linear weights. Pure offense, before comparing to anyone.

BaselineThe average bar

The average runs-per-turn for this roster — the line everyone is measured against. There's one baseline for hitting and one for pitching.

BatRABBatting Runs Above Baseline

How many runs better (or worse) than the team's average hitter. Positive = above average bat, negative = below.

PosRunsPosition Runs

A fairness adjustment for how hard the kid's position is. Catchers and shortstops get runs added; first basemen and DHs get runs subtracted — because the same bat is worth more from a tough position.

PitchRABPitching Runs Above Baseline

For pitchers only: how many runs they saved compared with a baseline pitcher. Allow fewer runs than expected and this is positive.

Replacement gapThe "spare-part" penalty

How much worse a replacement fill-in is than average. We add a little of this when setting the bar, so "replacement level" sits below average. Hitting and pitching have their own gaps.

Full-season PAA full season's at-bats

How many plate appearances count as a "whole season." Used to scale the position bonus so a kid who played half a season gets half the position credit.

PythagPythagorean exponent

A number from a famous baseball formula (by Bill James) that links runs to win percentage — named because it resembles the Pythagorean theorem. It quietly sets the runs-per-win exchange rate above.
