Is it actually a VaR?

Three ways of setting a daily 99% Value at Risk on $1,000,000 of the NASDAQ Composite, each put through the three backtests in Aaron Brown's “Forced by the Sternest Circumstances” (Wilmott, 2009). A break is a day the portfolio loses more than its VaR. There should be one in a hundred: no more, no fewer, and no pattern to when.

The three most important tests for a VaR methodology are:

  1. The actual fraction of VaR break days is 1 percent, within statistical tolerance;
  2. The VaR breaks are randomly distributed in time;
  3. The VaR breaks are independent of the level of VaR.
Aaron Brown, Wilmott magazine, July 2009

How this page is checked

  • Recomputed from scratch. A second program, sharing no code with the first, rebuilds every VaR series, break day, count and p-value on this page from the raw FRED file and compares them one by one.
  • Tested where the answer is known. On simulated returns with nothing to cluster, the historical-simulation rule has to break on 1% of days, as the article says it must, and it does. The usual spreadsheet percentile sits nearly a whole day less extreme and breaks too often even there, which would have overstated that method's failure on real data.
  • Faults planted on purpose. The checker is run against copies of this page with deliberate errors in them (a VaR series shifted by a day, two p-values swapped, a verdict flipped) and has to catch every one.

Data. FRED NASDAQCOM, Simple daily returns; dollar loss = −$1M × return. The first 750 trading days seed the three-year windows.
