Step 1 of 6

Three numbers are the whole model

Brown and Asness deliberately built the simplest model that could answer the question. They sliced a hockey game into 10-second intervals and asked: in any one slice, what is the chance each team scores? From the 2015–16 NHL season they needed exactly three numbers.

Your team scores in a slice, goalies in

0.65%

4,947 even-strength goals ÷ 126,425 minutes ÷ 6 slices per minute. Same for the opponent.

You score with your net empty (6 attackers)

1.97%

From 2,206 minutes of 6-on-5 play that season. Your offense triples.

They score into your empty net

4.30%

Their scoring rate multiplies 6.6× the moment your goalie steps off.

That's it. No power plays, no momentum, no home ice, two average teams — the paper's point is strategic insight, not in-game coaching. Everything that follows is bookkeeping on these three numbers.

Step 2 of 6

Measured in goals, pulling is madness

Look at the trade you're offered. Pull the goalie and your scoring chance per slice goes from 0.65% to 1.97% — roughly 3× your firepower. But the opponent's chance goes from 0.65% to 4.30% — 6.6× the danger.

Expected goal differential, per 100 slices with the net empty

+ 1.97 scored − 4.30 conceded = −2.33 goals

With goalies in, the same 100 slices are dead even: +0.65 − 0.65 = 0. Pulling is a strictly losing bet in goals — about a quarter of a goal given away per pulled minute.

By this arithmetic no one should ever pull a goalie, and the coach who waits until the final minute looks like the prudent one. This is the paper's first lesson, and it has nothing to do with hockey: before you measure the risk, make sure you picked the right thing to measure. Goals are the wrong measure.

Look at the pulled column's worst outcome. The empty-net goal you dread — the 4.30% — is multiplied by 0, because losing by two pays exactly what losing by one pays: nothing. In the final slice, pulling the goalie has no downside at all. You just watched a 6.6× increase in danger cost 0.000 points.

That is the whole trick. Pulling the goalie raises volatility and lowers the expected score — and it is still correct, because the thing being maximized (points) is an asymmetric function of the thing getting riskier (goals). Once you're losing, the downside is already spent.

Step 4 of 6

Now wind the clock backwards

At 10 seconds the answer was obvious. The paper's engine — backward induction — extends it to any time on the clock: the value of a situation at time t is the probability-weighted value of the three situations it can become at t − 10s. We already know everything at 0:10, so we can price 0:20. Still down one:

Where did 0.02955 come from? It's the pull total from Step 3 — each backward step assumes you keep playing optimally afterwards. And 1.4935 is the value of being tied with 0:10 left (goalie back in net, naturally): 98.70% × 1.5 + 0.65% × 2 + 0.65% × 0.

Notice the down-2 branch is still worth zero at 0:20 — ten more seconds isn't enough to make being two behind worth anything. So pulling still has almost no downside, and the recursion keeps favoring it… for a while. Repeat the step 360 times — a full game — and somewhere the balance must tip, because with lots of clock left, falling two behind does destroy real value. Watch for the tip:

Why 6:10? The insurance-pricing view

Compare pulling to waiting for one slice, and the recursion collapses to a single inequality. Pulling adds 1.32 points of firepower per hundred slices (1.97 − 0.65) and 3.65 points of danger (4.30 − 0.65). So:

pull ⇔ (value of tying) > 2.77 × (cost of falling two behind)

where 2.77 = 3.65 ÷ 1.32, the price of the extra danger per unit of extra firepower. Early in the game the cost of going down two is high and tying regains little of the game you'd still have anyway. As the clock drains, the cost of “down two” decays toward zero while tying stays precious:

At 6:10 — and not a moment earlier — the insurance gets cheap enough to buy. An options trader would say it differently: a trailing team holds an out-of-the-money call on the tie, and pulling the goalie is a lever that buys volatility. Volatility is unambiguously good only when the option is nearly dead. The crossover is where “nearly dead” begins.

Why a fund manager wrote this

The same mistake, in a portfolio

The authors' actual subject was never hockey. Each piece of the calculation above is a rehearsal for a decision investors face with real money:

1 · Measure the risk that pays

Pulling the goalie raises goal volatility and lowers expected goals — yet lowers the risk of losing. Investors who judge an asset by its own volatility, instead of by what it does to the whole portfolio's chance of an unacceptable outcome, are scoring the wrong game — exactly like a coach maximizing goal differential.

2 · Volatility can be insurance

A trailing team holds an out-of-the-money option on the game, and an option holder near expiry wants volatility, even at negative expected return. Pulling the goalie is buying volatility when convexity makes it cheap — the asymmetric payoff does the work.

3 · Failing conventionally is safer — for you

Coaches aren't rewarded for winning; they're rewarded for being seen as good coaches. Losing 1–2 after a quiet last-minute pull is forgivable; losing 0–5 after pulling at 13:00 is career suicide. CIOs face the identical trap: the crash that hits everyone is excusable, the unconventional position that disappoints is not. That's why a free +4 points sat unclaimed for 80 years.

4 · Sins of commission get billed; omission is free

Nobody itemizes the points lost by not pulling at 6:10 — or the returns lost by the diversifying allocation never made. The visible risk of acting is priced against the invisible risk of doing nothing, and doing nothing wins the argument while losing the game.

“Worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally.” — J. M. Keynes, quoted by the authors as the moral of the whole exercise

Where the data comes from

The paper's inputs are aggregates from the 2015–16 NHL season: 4,947 even-strength goals over 126,425 even-strength minutes, and 2,206 minutes of play with one net empty and both teams otherwise at full strength. To rebuild or update them yourself, the standard sources for situation-split (5v5, 6v5, empty-net) goals and time-on-ice are Natural Stat Trick, MoneyPuck (free CSV downloads including empty-net flags per shot), Evolving-Hockey, Hockey-Reference, and the NHL Stats API. None of that is needed to follow this page: the model requires only the three headline probabilities, and the accompanying pull_goalie.py also validates the whole analysis on synthetic seasons it generates itself (--simulate 200000).

The model, precisely

State: (score differential S, time remaining t), 10-second steps, terminal payoff 0 / 1.5 / 2 for behind/tied/ahead. Three value functions solved backwards from t = 0: EPNP (never pull), EPNO (goalie in for this slice, optimal after), EPPO (net empty for this slice, optimal after), with V = max(EPPO, EPNO) as the continuation value. Policy: pull wherever EPPO > EPNO. The opponent is assumed never to pull, as in the paper. Full derivation and the Monte Carlo validator are in the Python file; this page re-solves the identical recursion in JavaScript on every slider move.
