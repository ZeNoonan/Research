A ten-minute guide to generals.io & the Generals bot competition

Generals in Ten Minutes

Two crowns on a foggy grid. Numbers tick upward. Whoever walks onto the other crown first takes everything. Read the rules, then play a full game against the reference bot — right here on this page.

Engine: strakam/generals-bots Rules verified against the JAX source Sept 2026

The whole game

One sentence, then the details

You own tiles; tiles grow armies; you push armies into neighbouring tiles to take them; you win by stepping onto the enemy general.

That's it. Everything else — fog, castles, the arithmetic of a clash — is detail on top of those four clauses. The whole game is a single 5-integer decision, repeated a few hundred times, under fog.

Generals.io is the original browser game. generals-bots is a JAX reimplementation of it built to train bots — it runs 10 million game steps a second, and it powers the Generals Competition, where the players are programs rather than people. The rules below are read straight out of that engine.

The number on a tile is its army — how many units are standing there. It's the only quantity in the game.

Play it

You are red. The bot is blue.

Tap one of your tiles, then tap a neighbour to send armies there. On a keyboard use the arrow keys — they queue a whole path, one step per turn, exactly like the real game. Your first move starts the clock.

The opening everyone plays: for the first fifty turns nothing much grows, so spend them walking your general's army outward in a long line and grabbing empty land. Land is what compounds. Then come back for a castle.

Rule one

Where armies come from

Generals and castles: +1 every other turn

Every general and every castle you own gains one army on each even turn — so roughly one army every two turns, forever. A castle is a second general that can't lose you the game.

turn 100 general 51 turn 102 general 52 turn 104 general 53

Every tile you own: +1 every fifty turns

On turns 50, 100, 150 … every tile you own gains one army. This is the whole economy: at turn 200 a player holding 60 tiles banks 60 armies at once, and a player holding 20 banks 20.

land 60 → +60 army at turn 250 land 20 → +20 army at turn 250

Nothing else generates armies

No production, no upgrades, no resources. Land and structures, that's the entire income statement — which is why the early game is a land grab and the late game is arithmetic.

Rule two

How a move works

One move per turn, into an orthogonally adjacent tile. You always leave one army behind — a tile you own never empties itself completely.

Send all, or send half

Default sends everything but one. A split sends half, rounded down, and is how you keep a tile defended while still advancing.

tile has 9 send all → 8 leave, 1 stays tile has 9 send half → 4 leave, 5 stay

Into your own tile: the armies stack

Moving onto a tile you already own just adds the numbers. This is how you build one big fist out of a hundred scattered ones.

8 → [ 3 ] becomes 1 [ 11 ]

Into anyone else's tile: subtract

The two numbers cancel. Strictly more attackers than defenders and you take the tile with the difference standing on it. Equal or fewer and the defender holds, with the difference left behind.

8 attacks 3 → you take it, 5 army 3 attacks 8 → they hold it, 5 army 5 attacks 5 → they hold it, 0 army

Onto a general: you win

Take the enemy general and the game ends immediately — and every tile they owned becomes yours. A player who was ahead on land loses all of it in one move.

The consequence people miss: attacking costs you the defender's number, every time. Walking a 50-stack through five tiles of 1 army each arrives as a 45-stack. Territory is not free to cross.

Rule three

Fog of war

You see a tile only if you own it or it touches a tile you own — the eight neighbours of every tile you hold. Everything else is dark. Mountains and castles you've already walked past stay drawn as dim shapes, so the terrain is remembered even when the armies on it are not.

Note what the remembered shapes don't tell you. The engine hands a bot a single structures_in_fog grid — mountains and castles share it. A dark shape you walked past once could be either, and you find out by walking into it.

Two numbers are never hidden, though: the scoreboard always shows the opponent's total army and total land. You can't see where they are, but you always know how big they are. A bot that watches that number jump by 40 knows a castle just fell somewhere.

Fog is also why a long thin line of tiles is worth more than its army suggests — it's a searchlight. Turn fog off with the button on the board to see what the bot has been doing behind it.

The turn

What happens each tick, in order

  1. Both players submit one action. Nobody sees the other's before submitting.
  2. Move order is decided: a move chasing the opponent's source tile goes first; then a move that only reinforces your own tile; then the smaller army moves first.
  3. The first player's move resolves. Then the second player's — against the board the first one just left behind.
  4. An illegal action (no army, a mountain, a tile you don't own) is silently treated as a pass. It costs you the turn, not the game.
  5. The turn counter advances, then growth is applied: structures on even turns, all land every fiftieth.

Move order matters more than it looks. Two armies walking into each other resolve one at a time, so the order decides who is attacking and who is defending — and defending is cheaper.

And what it sends back

Five integers. That is the entire action space of the game.

[ pass, row, col, direction, split ]

  pass       0 = move   1 = skip the turn   2 = build a castle
  row, col   the tile the armies leave from
  direction  0 = up   1 = down   2 = left   3 = right
  split      0 = send all but one   1 = send half

[0, 4, 7, 3, 0]  →  from row 4 col 7, march right, send everything.
[1, 0, 0, 0, 0]  →  pass.

A game is 500–1200 of these. Everything a bot knows how to do has to be expressible as picking one, under fog, in a few milliseconds.

A real bot

The Expander, in about twenty lines

This is the reference agent that ships with the engine — the same one you just played against. It has no memory, no plan, and no idea where the enemy general is. Every turn it scores every legal move and takes the best:

# score every move out of every tile we own
for r, c in my_tiles:
    src = army[r][c]
    if src <= 1: continue            # one army always stays behind

    for d, (dr, dc) in enumerate(UP_DOWN_LEFT_RIGHT):
        nr, nc = r + dr, c + dc
        if off_board(nr, nc) or is_mountain(nr, nc): continue
        if src <= army[nr][nc] + 1: continue   # can't win the clash

        score = float(src)
        if not_mine(nr, nc):   score *= 10.0   # new land beats stacking
        if theirs(nr, nc):     score *= 2.0    # enemy land beats neutral

        best = max(best, (score, (0, r, c, d, 0)))

return best_move or any_legal_move or PASS

Three constants — 10, 2, and "biggest stack first" — and it will beat a person who hasn't played before. It also has an obvious hole: it never defends its general and never goes looking for yours, so it can be ahead on every number and still lose to one line drawn at its crown. The Hunter option on the board is the engine's answer to that — it keeps a garrison at home, sends only its surplus out as one advancing stack, and walks that stack at your general the moment it sees it.

That gap is the whole competition. Beyond it lies searching under fog, guessing where the general is from the scoreboard, and reinforcement learning on millions of self-play games.

Competition rules

How the bot competition differs

The competition preset changes three things about the game above. Switch the board to Competition rules to play it.

You build castles instead of finding them

No neutral castles are placed on the map. Instead a third action kind, [2, row, col, 0, 0], converts one of your own plain tiles into a castle — paid for out of the army standing on that tile.

cost = 35 + Σ max(0, 14 − 2 × distance) over your own structures open ground, nothing near 35 next to your own general 47

Crowding your own structures is expensive; anything seven tiles away is free of surcharge. Enemy structures never change your price. And building can leave the tile on zero army — a brand-new castle can be sniped by a single unit on the very next turn.

Deathtouch: from turn 800, armies stop mattering at the crown

Any legal move that lands on the enemy general wins instantly, however small. A 1-army straggler beats a 900-army garrison. Both players touching on the same turn is a draw.

The only defence is to capture the attacker's source tile before the move resolves — a counter-move onto it counts as a chase, so it goes first. Which is exactly why move order is a rule and not a detail.

Bigger maps, longer games, full fog

Rectangular boards drawn independently in 18–21 per side, about a quarter of it mountains, generals at least 17 steps apart walking around them, and a 1200-turn cap. Bots speak a line-based stdin/stdout protocol, so they can be written in any language.

board 18–21 × 18–21 mountains 24–26% cap 1200 turns deathtouch 800

Scaled for this page: the board here is 12 × 12 with a 500-turn cap, and deathtouch lands on turn 330 instead of 800 — the same proportion of the game, short enough to finish on a phone.
