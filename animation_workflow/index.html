    How does a 3D animated film actually get made?

    Follow one six-second shot — Pip the little robot leaping for a firefly — through every department of an animation studio. Watch it change from a scribble in a notebook into a finished, lit, rendered frame.

    The pipeline in one picture

    A studio works a bit like a car factory. Each department does one job really well, then passes its work along to the next — that chain of steps, people and software is called the pipeline. It falls into three phases: plan it, make it, finish it.

    Phase 1

    Pre-production

    Before anything is built in 3D, the whole film is planned with words and drawings — because changing a drawing costs minutes, while changing a finished shot can cost weeks.

    01 Pre-production

    Story — it starts with an idea

    Every animated film begins as words, not pictures. A director pitches an idea, writers shape it into a story and then a script, and the story keeps being rewritten — often for years — until it works.

    For this tour we’ll follow one tiny story, and one shot from it: Pip, a little robot, sees a firefly for the first time and leaps to catch it.

    Who
    Director, writers, story team
    Tools
    Paper, scriptwriting software, lots of meetings

    Passes on a script → visual development & story artists

    02 Pre-production

    Visual development — what does it look like?

    Concept artists draw and paint dozens of options: what the characters could look like, what the world looks like, which colours set the mood. The director picks a direction — here, the round, bouncy robot.

    Then a model sheet locks the design: the character from the front, side and back, lined up on guidelines, so every artist further down the line builds exactly the same Pip.

    03 Pre-production

    Storyboard — the film as a comic strip

    Story artists draw the script as quick sketches, panel by panel, like a comic. Each panel shows what the camera sees and what the characters do.

    They’re rough on purpose: if an idea doesn’t work it’s thrown away and redrawn in minutes. A feature film goes through tens of thousands of these drawings. Our shot — sq010_sh020, sequence 10, shot 20 — gets six panels.

    Who
    Story artists, head of story
    Tools
    Storyboard Pro, Photoshop

    Passes on storyboard panels → editorial

    04 Pre-production

    Animatic — a rough movie made of drawings

    The editor cuts the storyboard panels together with temporary voices, music and sound effects, timing each one. The result, an animatic, is the first time anyone can sit down and actually watch the film.

    This is where pacing gets fixed while it’s still cheap. It also fixes each shot’s exact length: ours runs 6 seconds, and at 24 frames per second that’s 144 frames that every department now has to deliver.

    Who
    Editors, director
    Tools
    Avid Media Composer, Premiere Pro

    Passes on the cut, with every shot’s frame count → all departments

    Phase 2a

    Production: build the assets

    An asset is any “thing” in the film: a character, a prop, a set. Each one is built once, on its own, then reused in every shot it appears in. Asset artists usually work on a neutral turntable, far from any particular shot.

    05 Production · assets

    Modelling — sculpting in 3D

    A modeller builds Pip as a 3D surface made of thousands of tiny flat faces called polygons — like a sculpture made of paper triangles. More polygons means smoother curves, but heavier files that are slower for everyone to work with.

    Pip is grey on purpose. With no colour to distract you, it’s easier to judge the shape. Models are reviewed spinning on a turntable like this one.

    06 Production · assets

    Surfacing — paint and materials

    The model is like an unpainted statue. Surfacing artists give it a skin in three steps:

    1. UVs. The 3D surface is unwrapped into flat pieces, like a cardboard box opened out flat. A labelled grid shows if anything is stretched.
    2. Painting. Colour, scuffs, panel lines and that “07” on Pip’s back are painted onto the flat images.
    3. Materials. Rules for how light behaves on each surface: shiny plastic, brushed metal, a glossy glass visor, glowing eyes.

    07 Production · assets

    Rigging — bones and puppet strings

    Right now Pip is a statue; it can’t move. A rigger builds a digital skeleton inside it (the yellow bones) and wires it to controls, the coloured circles animators grab — a bit like the strings on a puppet.

    Riggers also add cartoon tricks like squash and stretch, so Pip can bend like a rubber ball rather than a stiff toy. The audience never sees a rig, but it decides how good the animation can be.

    Phase 2b

    Production: make the shots

    Now the assets come together, shot by shot. A feature film usually has more than a thousand shots, and each one passes through these same departments, one after another, while hundreds of others are at different steps at the same time.

    08 Production · shots

    Layout — where’s the camera?

    Layout artists are the film’s camera crew. Using the storyboard as a guide, they place the set, the characters and a virtual camera in 3D, choosing the lens, the angle and how the camera moves. Our camera drifts right and slowly pushes in as Pip lets the firefly go.

    They work with simple stand-in models and rough poses so everything stays fast. The inset shows exactly what the camera will see.

    09 Production · shots

    Animation — bringing Pip to life

    Animators don’t film anything. They pose the rig at key moments — keyframes, the diamonds on the timeline — and the computer fills in the frames in between. They work in passes:

    1. Blocking. Only the main poses, held like a flip-book of statues. Is the acting right?
    2. Spline. The computer smooths the motion between poses; the animator refines the timing.
    3. Polish. The details that make it feel alive: the antenna wobbling after the landing, blinks, breathing.

    10 Production · shots

    FX — things nobody animates by hand

    Dust, sparks, smoke, water, fire, cloth, hair: these are far too fiddly to pose by hand. FX artists set up rules such as gravity, wind and how fast things fade, and let the computer simulate thousands of particles.

    Switch to the raw view to see what an FX artist sees: just dots. Each one is a particle the simulation tracks: the firefly’s glowing trail, dust when Pip takes off and lands, sparkles as it’s let go, and other fireflies in the dark.

    11 Production · shots

    Lighting — painting with light

    For the first time the whole scene appears with its final materials, and lighting artists place virtual lights just like a cinematographer on a film set. Lighting sets the mood and tells your eye where to look. For this dusk shot:

    • Key cool moonlight, the main light, casting the shadows.
    • Fill soft purple glow from the sky, so shadows aren’t pitch black.
    • Rim warm sunset from behind, outlining Pip against the background.
    • Practical the firefly itself, lighting Pip’s face when its hands open. The whole shot is built around that moment.

    Phase 3

    Post-production

    Until now everyone has worked with quick previews. Now the computer makes the real, full-quality pictures, and they get polished and finished.

    12 Post-production

    Rendering — computing every pixel

    Rendering makes the final image: the computer traces how light bounces around the scene to work out the colour of every single pixel. Production renderers draw the frame in small squares called buckets, as in the viewport. At film quality one frame can take hours, and every second of film needs 24 of them.

    So studios use a render farm, rooms full of computers (often thousands) each rendering different frames at the same time. Each frame is also saved as separate passes that give the next department extra control.

    Try it — look at the passes

    The render farm: our 144 frames

    If each frame took 2 hours on one computer, this shot would take 12 days. Spread across 100 machines it’s done in under 3 hours.

    13 Post-production

    Compositing — layering it all together

    Compositors stack the rendered layers like layers in Photoshop and do the final polishing that’s quicker on images than re-rendering: the glow around the firefly, colour tweaks, a soft vignette. If the director wants the sky a touch warmer, it’s changed here in minutes rather than re-rendered for hours.

    14 Post-production

    The finished shot

    Finished shots go back to the editor and replace the storyboard drawings in the cut, one by one, until the whole film is final. Sound designers add every footstep, whirr and chirp, the composer records the score, and a colourist grades the film so every shot matches. Then it’s delivered to cinemas and streaming services.

    Our six-second shot passed through fourteen steps and dozens of hands. Multiply that by more than a thousand shots and you have a feature film.

    It’s not really a straight line

    The tour went department by department, but a real studio is busier and loopier than that.

    Dailies

    Every day, artists show their work in progress to the director and supervisors, who give notes. A single shot can go round this loop dozens of times before it’s approved.

    Notes flow upstream

    Animation might reveal the rig needs a new control; lighting might show a texture looks wrong. The work goes back, gets fixed and flows down again. That’s why every file has a version number like _v023.

    Everything at once

    While shot 20 is being lit, shot 21 is being animated and the next sequence is still being storyboarded. Like an assembly line, every station is busy with a different shot.

    The plumbers

    Pipeline TDs and engineers write the software that moves files between departments and tracks every version, so when a modeller fixes Pip, every shot picks up the fix.

    By the numbers

    24frames for every second of film
    144frames in our six-second shot

    Glossary

    Asset
    Any reusable “thing” in the film: a character, prop, vehicle or set.
    Sequence & shot
    A sequence is a run of story (roughly a scene); a shot is one continuous piece of camera footage between two cuts.
    Frame
    A single still image. Film plays 24 of them every second.
    Polygon
    One of the tiny flat faces a 3D model’s surface is made of.
    UVs
    The “unfolded” flat version of a 3D surface that textures are painted onto.
    Shader / material
    The rules for how light reacts to a surface: shiny, rough, metallic, glowing, see-through.
    Rig
    The skeleton and controls that let animators move a model.
    Keyframe
    A pose the animator sets at a specific frame. The computer fills in the in-betweens.
    Playblast
    A quick, low-quality preview of a shot, used to review animation without rendering.
    Simulation
    Letting the computer calculate motion using physics-like rules, as FX does for dust and sparks.
    Render / render farm
    Computing the final images; a farm is the large group of computers that does it.
    Pass (AOV)
    One layer of a render, such as depth or just the lighting, kept separate for compositing.
    Dailies
    The daily review where work in progress is shown and notes are given.
    Pipeline TD
    A technical director who builds the tools and plumbing that connect departments.

    Pip, the meadow, the textures, the animation and the effects are all generated in code and rendered live with three.js, with no video files. Studios differ in the details; this is the common shape of a 3D feature pipeline.
